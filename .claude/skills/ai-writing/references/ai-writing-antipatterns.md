# AI Writing Anti-Patterns: Complete Reference

Distilled from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI Writing" article and supporting linguistic research
(Kobak et al. 2025, Geng & Trotta 2025, Juzek & Ward 2026, Merrill et al. 2025).
This is the full catalogue that the ai-writing skill draws from. Each pattern includes
the category, the tell, and brief guidance on how to avoid it.

## Content Patterns

### Significance inflation
AI text puffs up the importance of mundane subjects by connecting them to "broader trends,"
"lasting legacies," or "pivotal moments." A statistical institute becomes "a pivotal moment
in the evolution of regional statistics." A small-town road becomes proof that the town
"solidified its role as a regional hub."

**How to avoid:** State facts plainly. If something is important, the facts demonstrate it
without needing a sentence announcing its importance. Delete any sentence whose primary
purpose is to assert that something matters.

### Notability assertion
AI text painstakingly emphasizes media coverage and sources in body text, listing outlets
by name to prove the subject is noteworthy: "featured in Wired, Vogue, and CNN." Human
writers cite sources as footnotes, not as proof of importance within the prose itself.

**How to avoid:** If you mention coverage, use it to convey information ("the New York Times
reported the deal was worth $4.2B"), not to assert notability ("has been covered by the
New York Times and other major outlets").

### Superficial analysis via trailing participles
Sentences that end with "-ing" phrases offering vague analysis: "...highlighting the
importance of regional cooperation" or "...reflecting broader trends in digital
transformation." These phrases add no information — they just sound analytical.

**How to avoid:** If the analysis matters, make it a standalone sentence with specific
evidence. If it doesn't matter, delete it.

### Promotional and travel-guide tone
AI adopts a press-release, travel-brochure, or advertisement tone even on mundane topics.
A town becomes "nestled in the heart of" a region with "natural beauty" and a "diverse
array" of attractions. A company "exemplifies a commitment to" something or other.

**Words to watch:** boasts a, nestled, in the heart of, natural beauty, diverse array,
renowned, groundbreaking, exemplifies, commitment to, rich (figurative — "rich cultural
heritage"), profound (figurative), gateway to, captivates, dynamic hub

**How to avoid:** State facts. If something is good, describe what makes it good rather
than labeling it with promotional adjectives. A town with good food has good food — it
doesn't "boast a vibrant culinary scene."

### Vague attributions
"Experts note," "researchers suggest," "critics argue," "scholars have observed" —
without naming anyone specific. AI uses these to simulate consensus.

**How to avoid:** Name the person. "MIT economist Daron Acemoglu argues..." If you can't
name someone, ask whether the claim needs an attribution at all.

### Source exaggeration
AI presents views from one or two sources as widely held: "several publications have cited"
when only two exist, or "many scholars agree" based on a single paper. It uses "such as"
before exhaustive word lists, implying more exist.

**How to avoid:** Match your attribution scope to your evidence. One source = "one study found."
Two sources = name them both. Don't pluralize what's singular.

### Knowledge-gap hedging
AI fills gaps in its training data with hedge phrases then speculates anyway:
"While specific details are limited," "not widely documented," "based on available
information," "details regarding... remain scarce." The pattern is: admit ignorance,
then offer vague filler as if it were analysis.

**How to avoid:** If you don't have specific information, don't pretend to hedge around it.
Either find the information or skip the claim entirely. Readers respect silence more than
filler.

### Conservation status boilerplate (domain-specific)
When writing about animals, plants, or species, AI tends to insert generic paragraphs about
conservation status, ecosystem roles, and preservation efforts — even when the species' status
is unknown and no specific efforts exist.

**How to avoid:** Only discuss conservation if you have specific, sourced information about it.

## Vocabulary Tells

### The Banned Word List (by research frequency)

These words appear at statistically anomalous rates in post-2022 AI text compared to
pre-2022 human text on the same topics:

**Tier 1 — Strongest tells (flagged across multiple studies):**
delve, underscore (verb), tapestry (figurative), intricate/intricacies, fostering,
showcase/showcasing, pivotal, meticulous/meticulously, bolstered, garner, interplay,
emphasizing, vibrant (abstract use), enduring

**Tier 2 — Strong tells:**
crucial, enhance, landscape (abstract), testament, align with, valuable (insights),
Additionally (sentence-initial), key (adjective), highlight (verb)

**Tier 3 — Moderate tells (context-dependent):**
comprehensive, robust (non-physical), navigate (figurative), leverage (verb),
Moreover (sentence-initial), Furthermore, Notably, It's worth noting,
In today's [anything], nestled, renowned, groundbreaking, profound (figurative),
rich (figurative — "rich cultural heritage"), diverse array, commitment to,
exemplifies, captivates

### Copula avoidance
AI systematically replaces "is/are/was/has" with fancier verbs. A 2024 study documented
a 10%+ decrease in "is" and "are" usage in academic writing after 2022, with no change
in their frequency before that.

**Specific replacements to watch for:**
- "is" → "serves as," "stands as," "marks," "represents"
- "has" → "boasts," "features," "offers"
- "was established in" → "was founded, marking a pivotal moment in"
- "was a candidate" → "ventured into politics as a candidate"

**How to avoid:** Use "is," "are," "was," "has" freely. They are the most human words
in the English language. Simplicity is a feature, not a weakness.

### Elegant variation (synonym cycling)
AI's repetition-penalty causes it to cycle through synonyms for the same concept:
"the artist" → "the protagonist" → "the key player" → "the eponymous character."
Guardian editors mocked this as the "popular orange vegetable" problem (avoiding
the word "carrot" in an article about carrots).

**How to avoid:** Call things by their name. Repeat the actual term. "The fence" is
better than rotating through "the barrier," "the enclosure," "the perimeter structure."

## Grammar and Syntax

### Negative parallelisms
AI overuses contrastive structures that clear up non-existent misconceptions:

- "Not just X, but also Y" — implies the reader assumed it was only X
- "Not X, but Y" — implies the reader assumed X
- "It's not about X — it's about Y" — formulaic reframing

**How to avoid:** Make the positive claim directly. Instead of "It's not just a tool —
it's a philosophy," just say "It's a philosophy" (or better yet, explain what you mean
rather than labeling it).

### Rule of three
AI defaults to triplet lists: "adjective, adjective, and adjective" or "phrase, phrase,
and phrase." It uses this structure to make shallow analysis appear comprehensive.

**How to avoid:** Vary list lengths. Use pairs, quartets, or quintets. If you catch
yourself writing three of anything, ask whether a different number would be more honest.

### Hedging preambles before claims
AI sometimes acknowledges that a subject is minor before asserting its importance anyway:
"Though relatively small, [town] has played a crucial role in..." or "While not widely
known, [subject] has had a significant impact on..." The pattern is always: hedge the
importance, then inflate it anyway. The hedge makes it worse, not better — it draws
attention to the disconnect between the claim and reality.

**How to avoid:** Either the subject is important (state why with evidence) or it's not
(don't pretend it is). Don't hedge and then inflate.

## Structural Patterns

### "Challenges and Future Outlook" endings
The most identifiable AI article structure: a final section beginning with "Despite its
[positives], [subject] faces challenges including..." followed by speculation about future
initiatives. Almost no human-written articles end this way.

**How to avoid:** End articles with a specific forward-looking detail, a callback to the
opening, a question for the reader, or simply the last important point. Do not create a
quarantined "challenges" section.

### Dictionary-entry openings
AI articles often open with a definition: "[Topic] refers to the [category] in which
[description]." or "[Article title] is a [noun phrase] that [does something]."

**How to avoid:** Open with a hook — an anecdote, a surprising fact, a question, a scene.
If definition is needed, work it in naturally after the hook.

### Treating titles as proper nouns
For articles with descriptive titles (like "List of songs about Mexico"), AI introduces
the title as if it were a standalone entity: "The 'List of songs about Mexico' is a
curated compilation of musical works..."

**How to avoid:** Describe the subject, not the article title.

## Formatting Tells

### Em dashes (banned)
AI uses em dashes far more frequently than human prose, especially in a formulaic
"punched up" style that mimics sales writing. It uses em dashes where humans would use
commas, parentheses, or colons. Em dashes are one of the most recognizable AI formatting
tells across all models.

**Rule:** Do not use em dashes at all. Replace every em dash with a comma, parenthetical,
colon, or period. If the sentence needs an em dash to work, the sentence needs rewriting.

### Title Case in headings
AI strongly defaults to capitalizing all major words in headings ("Global Context:
Critical Mineral Demand"). Human web writers overwhelmingly use sentence case
("Global context: critical mineral demand").

### Bold-first bullet points
AI tends to structure bullets as "**Bold term:** Explanation paragraph" in a glossary-
like format. This is especially common with "key layers," "key components," or "key
elements" framing. The pattern appears across all major AI models.

**How to avoid:** Use bullets for genuine lists. Write definitions as prose, not
bold-term-colon-explanation structures. If you catch yourself writing a list titled
"Key [anything]:" with bold-first bullets, restructure as narrative paragraphs.

### Emoji in headings
AI decorates section headings and bullet points with emoji, especially in instructional
or talk-page contexts. This is a strong tell in professional content.

### Unnecessary small tables
AI creates tables for data that would be clearer as prose, especially 2-3 row tables
with a single data column.

**How to avoid:** Use tables only when comparing 3+ items across 2+ dimensions.

### Curly/smart quotation marks
ChatGPT and DeepSeek typically produce curly quotes ("...") while most human web text
uses straight quotes ("..."). Note: Claude and Gemini typically do not produce curly quotes.

## Rhetorical Patterns

### Collaborative communication leakage
AI sometimes leaves traces of its conversational mode in article text. These phrases
are appropriate in chat but signal AI provenance in published articles.

**Words to watch:** "Let's explore," "Here's what you need to know," "Great question!,"
"I hope this helps," "Of course!," "Certainly!," "Would you like...," "is there anything
else," "let me know," "here is a," "more detailed breakdown," "Let's dive in"

**How to avoid:** Read the draft as if it were printed in a magazine. Any phrase that
sounds like a chatbot talking to a user should be cut.

### Subject line leakage
AI-generated text sometimes includes email-style subject lines that were part of the
original chat prompt: "Subject: Request for Review and Clarification."

### Instruction echo
AI may echo or paraphrase its own instructions in the output, especially when given
detailed specifications. The output reads like a checklist being fulfilled rather than
organic prose.

**How to avoid:** After drafting, re-read for any passage that sounds like it's
reporting compliance rather than communicating to a reader.

## Era-Specific Markers

AI vocabulary shifts over time as models are updated and companies adjust for known tells.

**2023 to mid-2024 (GPT-4 era):**
Additionally, boasts, bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner,
intricate/intricacies, interplay, key, landscape, meticulous, pivotal, underscore,
tapestry, testament, valuable, vibrant

**Mid-2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o era):**
align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting,
pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant

**Mid-2025 onward (GPT-5 era):**
emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing — plus notability/attribution patterns

Note: Different models have distinct tells. ChatGPT uses curly quotes; Claude does not.
ChatGPT overused "delve" heavily in 2023; usage dropped sharply by 2025. Detection
heuristics should account for which model and era the text may have come from.
